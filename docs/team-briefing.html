Defending the Build

What our system does, why it is built this way, and how to answer a judge who is trying to find out whether we actually understand it.

Ministry of Consumer Affairs Dept. of Consumer Affairs Software · Miscellaneous Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011

If you remember one sentence

We are not building a scanner. We are building an evidence pipeline — a photograph becomes a millimetre measurement, becomes a cited rule, becomes a report an enforcement officer can sign.

Every answer below is a variation on this sentence. If a question knocks you off balance, come back to it.

The system, in four parts

A photograph enters at the top and a signed report leaves at the bottom. Each part does one job and hands off to the next. Learn these four and you can place any question a judge asks.

01

The Ruler

scale.py

Finds the bank card the officer placed in the photo. Because every bank card on earth is exactly the same size, once we know how many pixels wide the card is, we know the real-world size of everything else in the picture.

Say it like thisIt converts pixels into millimetres. Without this step, nothing else in the system is legally meaningful.
02

The Eye

extract.py

Locates the blocks of text on the label, then reads them — the MRP, the net quantity, the manufacturer's address, the date. It only reports what the label says. It is never asked whether anything is legal.

Say it like thisThis is the only part that uses AI, and it only reads. It has no opinion about the law.
03

The Lawyer

engine.py + rules/lmpc-2011.yaml

Holds the Legal Metrology Rules as a written-down list, not as programming. It takes the facts from the first two parts and decides what passes, what fails, and what is exempt — citing the exact rule number every time.

Say it like thisThe law lives in a file a legal officer could read and edit. When the rules change, nobody has to rewrite the software.
04

The Clerk

report.py

Draws the findings back onto the photograph and produces a draft inspection report — every violation with its rule citation, the measured value, the required value, and the compounding amount, ready for an officer to confirm and sign.

Say it like thisThe output is not a score. It is evidence that has to survive a legal challenge.

The five things that make us different

Every other team will read the label and tick a checklist. These are the five answers to "so what?"

We measure in millimetres, not pixels

Rule 7(2) sets a minimum letter height in millimetres, and the threshold depends on the physical area of the package. You cannot check that from a photograph unless you recover real-world scale. Almost nobody will.

The law is data, not code

The rules changed in 2021 and twice in 2026, with more landing in July 2027. Our rule file carries the date each rule took effect, so we judge a package against the law as it stood on the day it was packed.

We get the exemptions right

A 5 gram sachet is exempt. Unless it is tobacco. Food packaging follows FSSAI, not this clause. A checker that flags an exempt package loses an inspector's trust in thirty seconds.

We cover e-commerce, not just shelves

Rule 6(10) requires the same declarations on an online listing. One officer checks one packet at a time; the same rule engine can check thousands of listings.

We refuse to guess

When we cannot establish scale, we return "measurement required" instead of a violation. An enforcement tool that overclaims is worthless in a compounding proceeding.

Questions judges actually ask

Tap to open. Read the answer in your own words afterwards — a memorised paragraph sounds memorised.

Isn't this just OCR?

No — OCR reads text, and roughly half the rules we check are not about what the label says. They are about how big it is, where it sits, and whether it contrasts with the background.

Rule 7(2) sets a minimum letter height in millimetres. Rule 8 requires empty space around the net quantity. Rule 9 requires the price to contrast with the background. No OCR system answers any of those questions.

What if the AI hallucinates a violation?

It can't, because the AI never decides anything. It only reads text off the label. Every legal conclusion comes from a rule file that a human wrote, and each finding cites its rule number.

So a wrong output traces to exactly one of two places — a misread field, or a misapplied rule — and both are inspectable. We can open the file and show you.

How can you possibly measure millimetres from a photograph?

The officer places a bank card in the frame. Every bank card in the world is exactly 85.60 by 53.98 millimetres — it's an ISO standard. Once we know how many pixels wide the card is, we know the true size of everything else in the photo.

It's also normal evidentiary practice: forensic photography has always included a physical scale in frame. And if there is no card, we say "measurement required" rather than guess.

Why a web app instead of a mobile app?

It installs to the home screen and opens the phone's camera, so for the officer it behaves like an app. But there is no app store and no device management — a state can deploy it to its whole inspectorate by sending a link.

The problem statement asks for a "web and/or mobile" application. A native app would not make a single measurement more accurate, because it's the same camera.

What happens when the law changes?

Nothing breaks. The rules are a data file, not programming — a legal officer edits a line.

And every rule carries the date it came into force, so we judge a package against the law as it stood on its date of packing. That matters legally: you cannot penalise a package manufactured before an amendment commenced.

How is this different from the food-scanner apps that already exist?

Those tell a consumer what is inside the food. We tell an enforcement officer whether the package itself breaks the Legal Metrology Rules — MRP declaration, net quantity, manufacturer's address, letter height, placement.

Different law, different user, different output. Ours is evidence for a compounding proceeding, not nutrition advice.

Won't it produce false positives?

That is exactly why exemptions are checked before any rule runs. A packet at or under 10 grams is exempt under Rule 26(a) — unless it is tobacco. Food packaging follows FSSAI rather than Rule 6(1)(a). Bidis need no date declaration.

If you flag a 5 gram sachet as non-compliant, a real inspector stops trusting the tool in thirty seconds. Getting the exemptions right is how we prove we read the law.

Are you replacing inspectors?

No. The system proposes; the officer adjudicates. Every finding is a draft an officer confirms and signs.

That isn't modesty, it's law — a machine cannot compound an offence. What we remove is the hours spent measuring by hand, not the judgement.

Does it work offline?

The measurement runs entirely on the device, so all the geometry checks work with no signal. Only the text reading needs a network.

That matters — inspections happen in markets and warehouses where connectivity is poor.

What about Hindi and regional languages?

We deliberately do not use a traditional OCR engine, because Indian packaging is multilingual, glossy and low-contrast, and that is precisely where OCR fails. We use a vision model for reading instead.

The geometry side doesn't care about script at all — a letter's height in millimetres is the same measurement in any language.

What is your training data?

We don't train a model, so we don't need a labelled dataset — which is a real advantage, because no such dataset exists for Indian packaging compliance.

The law is our dataset. The LMPC Rules 2011 encoded as rules. We validate by buying real products and measuring them with a caliper.

How would a government actually deploy this?

A state Legal Metrology department hosts it; officers open a link and install it to their phones. No procurement of devices, no app store approval.

We designed the inspection records to export into the format used by eMaap, the National Legal Metrology Portal that the Department of Consumer Affairs launched in December 2024 — so this feeds the system that already exists rather than creating a parallel one.

What can't it do yet?

Curved surfaces. The maths that recovers scale assumes a flat face, so on a bottle we return "undetermined" for the geometry rules rather than a number we can't stand behind. Unwrapping cylinders is on our roadmap.

Answering this one openly is worth more than dodging it. A team that knows its own limits reads as a team that has actually built something.

When you don't know the answer

This is the section that matters most. Judges probe until they find the edge — they are testing whether the team is real, not whether one person knows everything. A confident handoff reads as a functioning team. A bluff that collapses discredits everything you said before it.

It's a teammate's layer
"That's the vision side — [name] owns it. What I can tell you is what it hands to my layer: a set of measurements in millimetres with a confidence value attached."
We haven't measured it yet
"We haven't measured that yet. Our target is ±0.2 millimetres and it's written into our test plan — I'd rather give you the number after we've tested it than guess now."
It's deliberately out of scope
"We decided not to handle that in version one. Curved bottles return 'undetermined' rather than a wrong measurement — we'd rather return nothing than something an officer can't defend."
You genuinely don't understand the question
"Can I check I've understood — are you asking about X or about Y?"  Asking a clarifying question reads as competence. Answering the wrong question reads as panic.
The judge is right and you were wrong
"You're right, that's a gap. We'll look at it."  Then move on. Do not argue, and do not over-apologise.

Do not claim these

Each of these will be checked by someone in the room, and each has a true version that is nearly as strong.

Never say
"We're integrated with eMaap."  →  "Our records are export-ready for eMaap." There is no public eMaap API. A Ministry judge will know.
Never say
"Our AI detects violations."  →  "The AI reads the label; a deterministic rule engine decides." The first sentence throws away our best architectural argument.
Never say
"It's 99% accurate."  →  "Our target is ±0.2 mm on flat packages, and here is how we test it." Never quote a number nobody measured.
Never say
"It works on any package."  →  "Flat panels today; curved surfaces return 'undetermined' by design."
Never say
"It's already deployed."  →  "It runs today on real products we bought and measured." Claim exactly what you can demonstrate on the spot.

Who answers what

Assign one owner per layer before you walk in. When a question lands, the owner answers and everyone else stays quiet — a team that talks over itself looks like a team that hasn't built anything.
