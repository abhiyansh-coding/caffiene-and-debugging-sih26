Part 01

The problem we picked

Problem Statement 26034, set by the Department of Consumer Affairs under the Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food & Public Distribution.

Every packaged item sold in India — a biscuit packet, a bottle of oil, a bag of cement — is legally required to print a specific set of declarations on its label. This comes from the Legal Metrology Act, 2009 and the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011, which we will call the LMPC Rules from here on.

Nine things must appear: who manufactured or packed it and their full address, the country of origin if imported, the common name of the product, the net quantity, the month and year of manufacture, the maximum retail price, consumer care contact details including a phone number and an email address, the unit sale price, and the dimensions where size matters.

But the law goes considerably further than "these things must be printed". It also specifies how they must be printed — how tall the letters must be in millimetres, how much blank space must surround the net quantity, and whether the price stands out from the background. Those requirements are where this problem gets genuinely interesting, and we will come back to them.

Why this is a real problem, not a made-up one

Enforcement is done by state Legal Metrology departments — inspectors who physically visit shops, warehouses and factories. There are millions of products and a few thousand inspectors. Checking a label properly means reading nine declarations and, in principle, measuring letter heights with a ruler. Nobody does the measuring. So the rules about letter size and placement are effectively unenforced, and manufacturers know it.

Meanwhile the problem has grown a second half. Rule 6(10) of the LMPC Rules requires online listings to carry the same declarations as the physical package, and amendments in February and April 2026 added new obligations around country-of-origin display for imported goods, with provisions landing on 1 July 2027. An inspector can walk into a shop. Nobody has given them a way to walk into Amazon.

Why anyone should care

If the MRP is printed in letters too small to read, or the net quantity is buried in a crowded corner of the packet, the consumer cannot check what they are paying for or how much they are getting. The rules about size and placement exist precisely so the declarations cannot be technically present but practically invisible.

What happens when a violation is found

The outcome is not a rating or a score. It is a legal proceeding. The offence is either compounded — settled by paying a fixed sum — or prosecuted. The compounding amounts run from ₹2,000 for a retailer up to ₹50,000 for a manufacturer or importer.

That single fact shapes our entire design. Whatever our system outputs has to hold up when a manufacturer's lawyer disputes it. We are not building something that gives an opinion. We are building something that produces evidence.

Part 02

What we are building

One rule engine, two ways to feed it.

At the centre sits a piece of software that knows the LMPC Rules. You give it a set of facts about a package — what the label says, how big the letters are, how large the package is — and it tells you which rules pass, which fail, and which do not apply, citing the exact rule number every time. That is the engine, and it is the valuable part.

The engine cannot see. It never touches a photograph or a web page. Things that can see feed it, and we are building two of them.

Client one — the field scanner

An inspector standing in a shop photographs a package with their phone, with an ordinary bank card held flat against the label. The system works out the real-world size of everything in the photo, reads the declarations, measures the letter heights in millimetres, runs the rules, and produces an annotated photograph plus a draft inspection report.

Client two — the listing checker

A browser add-on that reads whatever product page the officer is already looking at on Amazon or Flipkart, pulls out the same declarations, and runs the same engine against them.

Why this shape matters

Rule 6(1) is the same law whether the declarations are printed on a wrapper or displayed in a web page. So the rules do not need writing twice. Only the part that reads differs — a camera in one case, a web page in the other.

That is why adding the second client costs us roughly 15% more work but roughly doubles the reach of the project. It is the single best structural decision in the design.

The sentence that captures it

We are not building a scanner. We are building an evidence pipeline — a photograph becomes a millimetre measurement, becomes a cited rule, becomes a report an officer can sign.

Part 03

Why we win

Roughly 500 teams can submit an idea against this problem statement. Almost all of them will build the same thing: photograph the label, read the text, tick off a checklist. Here is what we do that they will not.

We measure in millimetres, not pixels

Rule 7(2) sets a minimum letter height in actual millimetres — 1 mm on a small packet, up to 6 mm on a large one — and which threshold applies depends on the physical area of the package's front panel. You cannot check that from a photograph unless you can work out real-world size from the image. It is the hardest requirement in the problem statement, and it is the one most teams will quietly skip.

We check the geometry nobody else checks

Rule 8 requires blank space around the net quantity — at least one letter-height above and below, two letter-heights left and right. Rule 9 requires the price to contrast with its background. These are checks about shape and colour, not text, and they are strikingly effective to demonstrate: we draw the legally required empty rectangle on the photo and highlight whatever intrudes into it.

The law is data, not code

The rules changed in 2021, then twice in 2026, with more taking effect in July 2027. In our system the rules live in a file that reads like a list, not buried inside the program. When the law changes, someone edits the list. Better still, every rule records the date it came into force, so a package is judged against the law as it stood on the day it was packed — which is legally required, because you cannot penalise a package made before a rule existed.

We get the exemptions right

Rule 26 exempts packages of 10 grams or less — but not tobacco. Food packaging follows FSSAI rules rather than this clause. Bidis need no date. A checker that flags an exempt 5-gram sachet as illegal will be dismissed by a real inspector within thirty seconds. Handling exemptions correctly is the clearest possible proof that we actually read the law.

We refuse to guess

When the system cannot establish scale reliably — no card in the photo, or a curved bottle — it returns "measurement required" rather than inventing a violation. For a tool whose output ends up in a legal proceeding, knowing when to say nothing is a feature. An enforcement tool that overclaims is worse than useless.

Part 04

How it actually works

A photograph goes in at the top and a signed report comes out at the bottom. Four parts, each doing one job and handing on to the next.

01

The Ruler

scale.py — turns pixels into millimetres

This is the part that makes the whole project possible, so it is worth understanding properly.

The problem it solves

Photograph a packet and a letter might come out 40 pixels tall. Is that 2 millimetres or 5? There is no way to tell — it depends entirely on how close the camera was.

So we put something of known size in the shot. Every bank card in the world is exactly 85.60 by 53.98 millimetres, fixed by an international standard. If the card measures 1,000 pixels across the photo, then one pixel is 0.0856 mm, and that 40-pixel letter is 3.4 mm. The unknown becomes known.

There is a second job here. Photos are almost never taken perfectly square-on, so the packet appears as a skewed shape. Using the card's four corners — which we know form a perfect rectangle in reality — the software mathematically flattens the image as though it had been photographed straight on. The technical name is a homography. Think of it as un-tilting the picture.

This step also decides when to give up. If the card is lying on a table beside an upright box rather than held against the label, the two surfaces are at different angles and the measurement would be wrong. We detect that and refuse, rather than reporting a confident wrong number.

02

The Eye

extract.py — finds the text and reads it

This runs in two halves, deliberately split, and the split is one of our strongest technical arguments.

First, ordinary image processing finds where the text is — no AI involved. It darkens and thickens the image until each line of print merges into a solid blob, then records where those blobs sit. This is reliable and repeatable.

Second, those cropped regions are sent to an AI model, which reads what they say and identifies which is the MRP, which is the net quantity, which is the manufacturer's address.

Why split it this way

AI models are excellent at reading text, especially messy multilingual Indian packaging where traditional character-recognition software fails badly. But they are unreliable at saying precisely where something is on an image — ask for exact coordinates and they will approximate.

Ordinary image processing is the exact opposite: hopeless at understanding meaning, extremely precise about position.

So each does only the half it is good at. Our millimetre measurements come from pixel arithmetic, never from an AI's guess. The AI reads. It never decides anything.

03

The Lawyer

engine.py + rules/lmpc-2011.yaml — applies the law

The engine takes the facts assembled so far and produces findings. Each finding names the rule, the verdict, what was measured and what was required — for example: Rule 7(2) Table I — FAIL — measured 2.1 mm, required 2.5 mm.

Crucially, the rules themselves are not written in program code. They sit in a separate file, written as a structured list that a person can read and edit.

Two ways to teach software the law

The obvious way is to write instructions into the program: "if the price is missing, report a violation". This works until the law changes, at which point you need a programmer, a new version, and a redeployment.

The better way is to write the rules in a list the program reads when it runs. Changing the law becomes editing a line of text. A legal officer could do it.

We do the second — and we go one step further. Every rule records the date it came into force, so a package packed in 2024 is judged against the 2024 version of the law. That is not a nicety; penalising a package for breaking a rule that did not yet exist would be legally indefensible.

Before any rule runs, the engine checks exemptions. Is this under 10 grams? Is it tobacco, which loses that exemption? Is it a food product, where a different statute applies? Is it a bidi, which needs no date? Getting this ordering right is what separates a tool an inspector trusts from one they switch off.

04

The Clerk

report.py — produces the evidence

Two things come out. First, the photograph with the findings drawn onto it: each declaration boxed and labelled with its rule, failures marked in red with measured against required, and the Rule 8 clear-space rectangle drawn around the net quantity so an intrusion is visible at a glance.

Second, a draft inspection report laid out like an official memo — a findings table with a rule citation on every row, the applicable compounding amount, the photographic evidence, and a signature block.

It is marked throughout as a draft awaiting officer confirmation. That is deliberate and legally necessary: the system proposes, the officer decides. Software cannot compound an offence.

Part 05

The stack

"Stack" just means the set of tools the software is built from. Ours is deliberately small, because there are two of us writing code and the fastest way to fail is to build four things badly instead of one thing well.

Why there is no database

This surprises people, so here is the reasoning. A database is a filing system for when you have a great many records and many people writing at once. We have one inspector taking one photograph. An inspection is simply a folder:

original.jpgThe photograph exactly as taken. Never modified — this is the evidence.
rectified.jpgThe un-tilted version used for measuring.
annotated.jpgThe photo with findings drawn on.
facts.jsonWhat was found on the label and how big it was.
findings.jsonWhich rules passed, failed, or did not apply.
The evidence argument

Because the original photograph sits untouched in its own folder and nothing ever writes back to it, we can prove the image was not altered after capture. A database that updates rows in place makes that harder to demonstrate, not easier.

When we genuinely need searching and history, the answer is SQLite — a database that is a single file, needs no server, and ships with Python already. Not a heavyweight database that would drag in cloud hosting we do not need.

What it costs to run

About ₹2 per package scanned, which is the cost of the AI reading the label. Demo results are saved to disk, so rehearsing costs nothing and works with no internet at all. Only the reading step needs a network — every measurement runs on the device.

Part 08

Limits and honesty

Knowing what our system cannot do is part of understanding it. These are also the five ways to lose a room, each paired with the true version — which is almost always nearly as strong.

Never say
"We're integrated with eMaap." → "Our records are export-ready for eMaap." There is no public interface. A judge from the ministry will know.
Never say
"Our AI detects violations." → "The AI reads the label; a rule engine decides." The first version throws away our best architectural argument.
Never say
"It's 99% accurate." → "Our target is ±0.2 mm on flat packages, and here is how we test it." Never quote a number nobody measured.
Never say
"It works on any package." → "Flat panels today; curved surfaces return 'undetermined' by design."
Never say
"It's already deployed." → "It runs today on real products we bought and measured." Claim only what you can show on the spot.
Part 09

Questions you will get

From judges, from teammates, from anyone you explain this to. Read the answers, then say them in your own words.

Isn't this just OCR?

No. OCR reads text, and roughly half the rules we check are not about what the label says — they are about how big it is, where it sits, and whether it contrasts with the background.

Rule 7(2) sets a minimum letter height in millimetres. Rule 8 requires blank space around the net quantity. Rule 9 requires the price to contrast with its background. No OCR system answers any of those.

What if the AI gets it wrong?

The AI only reads text. It never decides whether anything is legal. Every legal conclusion comes from the rule file, and each finding cites its rule number.

So a wrong result traces to exactly one of two places — a misread field or a misapplied rule — and both can be opened and inspected. That is very different from a system where an AI produces a verdict nobody can explain.

How can you measure millimetres from a photograph?

A bank card in the frame. Every bank card is exactly 85.60 by 53.98 millimetres by international standard, so once we know how wide the card is in pixels, we know the true size of everything else in the picture.

It is also standard evidentiary practice — forensic photography has always included a physical scale in shot. And with no card, we say "measurement required" rather than guess.

Why a web app instead of a mobile app?

It installs to the home screen and opens the phone's camera, so to the officer it behaves like an app. But there is no app store and no device management, so a state can deploy it to an entire inspectorate by sending a link.

The problem statement asks for "web and/or mobile". And a native app would not improve a single measurement, because it is the same camera either way.

What happens when the law changes?

Someone edits the rule file. Nothing is rebuilt and no programmer is needed.

And because every rule records when it took effect, a package is judged against the law as it stood on its packing date — which matters, because you cannot penalise a package made before a rule commenced.

How is this different from the food scanner apps that already exist?

Those tell a consumer what is inside the food. We tell an enforcement officer whether the package breaks the Legal Metrology Rules — the MRP declaration, the net quantity, the manufacturer's address, the letter height, the placement.

Different law, different user, different output. Ours is evidence for a legal proceeding, not nutrition advice.

Won't it produce false positives?

That is exactly why exemptions are checked before any rule runs. A packet of 10 grams or less is exempt under Rule 26(a) — unless it is tobacco. Food packaging follows FSSAI. Bidis need no date declaration.

Flag a 5-gram sachet as illegal and a real inspector stops trusting the tool immediately. Getting exemptions right is how we prove we read the law rather than skimmed it.

Are you replacing inspectors?

No. The system proposes, the officer decides, and every finding is a draft an officer confirms and signs. That is not modesty — software cannot compound an offence.

What we remove is the hours of manual measuring that currently means nobody checks letter sizes at all.

Does it work without internet?

All the measurement runs on the device, so every geometry check works offline. Only the text reading needs a network.

This matters in practice — inspections happen in markets and warehouses where signal is poor.

What about Hindi and regional languages?

We deliberately avoid traditional character-recognition software, because multilingual, glossy, low-contrast Indian packaging is exactly where it fails. We use an AI model for reading instead.

And the measurement side does not care about script at all — a letter's height in millimetres is the same measurement in any language.

What is your training data?

We do not train a model, so we need no labelled dataset — which is a genuine advantage, because no such dataset exists for Indian packaging compliance and building one would take longer than the competition.

The law is our dataset. We validate by buying real products and measuring them with a caliper.

What can't it do yet?

Curved surfaces. The scale-recovery maths assumes a flat face, so on a bottle we return "undetermined" for the geometry rules rather than a number we cannot stand behind. Unwrapping cylinders is on the roadmap.

Answering this openly is worth more than dodging it — a team that knows its own limits reads as a team that has actually built something.

Part 10

Glossary

LMPC Rules
Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011. The regulation our system checks against.
Legal Metrology
The law of weights and measures — that what is declared on a package matches what is inside, and is declared properly.
Declaration
Something the law requires to be printed on a package: MRP, net quantity, manufacturer's address, and so on.
Principal display panel
The front face of a package — the side a shopper sees. Its area determines the minimum letter height.
Compounding
Settling an offence by paying a fixed sum instead of going to court. ₹2,000 to ₹50,000 here.
Exemption
A case where the rules do not apply — packages of 10 g or less, restaurant food, certain medicines.
eMaap
The National Legal Metrology Portal, launched by the Department of Consumer Affairs in December 2024 to unify state departments.
Stack
The set of tools and languages a piece of software is built from.
Engine
Here, the part that applies the rules to facts and produces findings. It never touches images.
Client
Something that feeds the engine — our camera scanner and our browser add-on are both clients.
Homography
The mathematics that un-tilts a photograph taken at an angle, so it looks as if shot straight on.
OCR
Optical Character Recognition — older software that converts pictures of text into text. It struggles with Indian packaging.
YAML
A plain-text format for writing structured lists that both people and programs can read. Our rules live in one.
PWA
Progressive Web App — a website that installs onto a phone with its own icon and works offline.
API
The way one piece of software talks to another. We use one to send label crops to the AI model.
Repository
The shared folder holding all our code and documents, kept on GitHub so everyone has the same version.
